Calibration Analysis

PIT diagnostics & coverage calibration · 10 models · 39 quantile levels · n = 7,636

Best MCE: Hybrid (0.0086)
What is PIT?
The Probability Integral Transform (PIT) maps each observed outcome through the model's predictive CDF. Under perfect calibration, PIT values are Uniform(0,1) — a flat histogram at density = 1.0 (red dashed line). U-shaped = overconfident (intervals too narrow). Hump-shaped = underconfident (intervals too wide).
KS Test
Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic vs Uniform(0,1). Lower KS = closer to ideal calibration.
KS < 0.03 good KS 0.03–0.06 moderate KS > 0.06 poor
Coverage Calibration
For a perfectly calibrated model, the fraction of observations below the predicted α-quantile should equal α at every level. The plot shows empirical vs nominal coverage for all 39 quantile levels. Lines close to the diagonal indicate well-calibrated uncertainty estimates.
Mean Calibration Error (MCE)
MCE = mean absolute deviation of empirical from nominal coverage across all 39 levels. Lower = better. Hybrid and QRF achieve MCE below 0.009 on this dataset. Neural network models (BNN, ENS, MCD) show systematically higher MCE.

Adaptive Coverage Analysis

90% coverage = proportion of test patients whose true LOS fell inside the model's 90% prediction interval · X-axis: cases ranked into 10 deciles by predicted interval width (Decile 1 = narrowest/most confident → Decile 10 = widest/most uncertain) · 10 models · n = 7,636

Most adaptive: Ensemble DRF (+6.2pp)
What is Adaptive Coverage?
Cases are sorted into 10 deciles by their predicted interval width — a proxy for model-perceived uncertainty. Decile 1 = narrowest (most confident); Decile 10 = widest (most uncertain). An adaptive model should yield increasing coverage as uncertainty grows: it honestly widens its intervals for hard cases.
How to interpret
Adaptive ↑ Coverage increases with width — model is self-aware.
Anti-adaptive ↓ Coverage decreases — wider intervals are less reliable.
Flat ~ No systematic trend — uncertainty signal is uncorrelated.
Metric: OLS Slope β
β = linear regression slope across all 10 deciles (% coverage per decile). Captures the overall trend rather than just end-to-end difference. Threshold: β > +0.3 = Adaptive, β < −0.3 = Anti-adaptive, otherwise Flat. Target coverage = 90% (red dashed line).

Missingness Sensitivity Analysis

CRPS % change relative to complete-data baseline under MCAR · 3 missingness levels · 10 models · 50 replicates

Most robust: BNN (+5.69% at 30%)
Experimental Setup
Features were masked under Missing Completely At Random (MCAR) at rates of 10%, 20%, and 30%. Each rate was replicated 50 times. Missing values were imputed using median imputation before re-running each model. CRPS is compared to the complete-data baseline.
Metric
CRPS % change = (CRPSmissing − CRPSbaseline) / CRPSbaseline × 100.
Positive values indicate degradation. Lower is better — a robust model maintains predictive accuracy under missing data.

Results · Predictive Density

Predictive Density Analysis

Average predicted density vs empirical LOS distribution · 10 models · test set n = 7,636 · x ∈ [0, 21] days

Best CRPS: Hybrid (1.0499)
How densities are constructed
For each test patient, the 39-quantile prediction grid is converted to a smooth density via spline-differentiation of the quantile function (Q'(τ)⁻¹). Individual densities are then averaged across all 7,636 patients to produce the mean predictive density shown here.
What to look for
Grey fill = empirical LOS distribution (kernel density, repeated in each panel).
A well-fitting model's density should align closely with the grey reference — correct mode location, appropriate tail weight, and no systematic shift.
Key patterns
Tree-based models (DRF, RF, QRF) tend to produce sharper, more peaked densities. Bayesian/ensemble neural models (BNN, MCD, ENS) show broader, heavier-tailed distributions. Hybrid and Ensemble DRF combine both regimes.
⚠ Interpretation Note — DRF Paradox
DRF produces a marginal predictive density that closely matches the empirical LOS distribution, yet achieves the worst CRPS (1.1202) of all models. This apparent contradiction reflects a fundamental distinction:
Marginally correct ≠ Conditionally accurate. The average density (shown here) integrates out all covariate information. DRF's MMD-based splitting criterion targets distributional heterogeneity across the full covariate space (Ćevid et al., JMLR 2022), which can excel at reproducing the marginal shape while producing poorly-sharpened conditional intervals for individual patients — exactly what CRPS penalises. A model can look right on average yet be conditionally wrong case by case.
